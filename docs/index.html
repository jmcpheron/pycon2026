How it works

Five compound gears in a chain. Four meshes between them. Each mesh is a 4:1 reduction, so the whole chain multiplies to 4⁴ = 256 : 1. Spin the input gear with your thumb and the output gear moves about a quarter of a degree.

Five gears, four reductions — not five reductions. An idler-style chain of the same five gears would still only reduce 4:1 total.

There are six short explainer pages if you want the longer version. Start with The card in 3D.

  • Gear ratios — compound 4:1 multiplication with a thumb-travel intuition: ~20.3 m of finger motion per output rotation.
  • Decoding the gears from STEP — slice the committed STL, FFT the radial profile, recover tooth count and module from geometry alone.
  • Stacking — why a 3-level cycle of hub heights keeps the card thin without the discs colliding.

How it gets built

The card lives in a free public Onshape document. The STEP export is committed at the repo root. A little Click CLI called cardlab does five small things to it, leaning on build123d and cascadio:

cardlab inspect
Print AP242 schema, units, product structure, and tessellation stats.
cardlab build
Convert STEP to STL or GLB (format inferred from --out extension). The GLB path uses cascadio and preserves Onshape's per-part colors.
cardlab render
Render STEP to PNG by tessellating, then handing the result to OpenSCAD (iso / top / edge / front / right camera presets).
cardlab explode
Pull a STEP assembly apart into per-part PNG / GLB plus exploded views — an animated GIF, an exploded GLB, and a TOML manifest.
cardlab assemble
Glue multiple STEP parts back into a single STEP via a TOML manifest.

Everything under docs/assets/card/ is regenerated by the Build Card workflow on any push that touches the STEP or the cardlab script, and then auto-committed back. Push a new Onshape export and the hero GIF above rebuilds itself within a minute.

Try it yourself

$ git clone https://github.com/jmcpheron/pycon2026
$ cd pycon2026
$ uv sync --extra step
$ uv run cardlab inspect jmcpheron-card.step
$ uv run cardlab explode --in jmcpheron-card.step --out /tmp/card/
# → /tmp/card/{exploded.gif, jmcpheron-card.glb, parts/*.{stl,glb,png}, manifest.toml}

The step extra is opt-in — the OpenCascade wheel is ~180 MB. You'll need Python 3.11+, OpenSCAD on your $PATH, and uv. Or just open the Onshape doc and spin it in your browser — no install required.

Why I'm bringing this to PyCon

Mostly: I wanted something concrete to put in someone's hand and have a conversation about. A printed card with little gears inside is a much better opener than "hi, I work in software." If you got here from a Hacker News post — hi too. Find me on the floor at PyCon, or open an issue on the repo. Here's the laundry list of things I'd love to talk about:

  • Open-source licensing for physical 3D files. I picked CC BY-SA 4.0 for the geometry and MIT for the code (see the notice). Was that the right call? Copyright on mechanical designs is fuzzy; CERN OHL exists; whether CC even applies cleanly to STEP / STL is debated. I'd love to hear from people who've thought harder about this.
  • Sharing STEP files through open source. Who owns the canonical export when the source is a hosted Onshape doc? What does long-term custodianship look like for a file format that's already shifted through AP203 → AP214 → AP242?
  • 3D design in general. What tools, what workflows, what makes the experience suck for someone walking in cold from a software background?
  • Python for poking at STEP files. build123d for new construction, cascadio for colored GLB export, FFT-decoding tooth counts from an STL slice — the "geometry doesn't carry its own design intent" problem.
  • Onshape → other platforms. Is there a viable OSS / on-prem path for hobbyists? FreeCAD, Solvespace, build123d-as-source. What does "exportable design intent" even mean when the source tool is the cloud?
  • Gear ratios as a teaching tool. The math is fun, and the thumb-travel intuition — about 20 metres of finger motion per output rotation — is the kind of fact that makes someone curious about the rest.
  • Print-in-place mechanisms. Geometry tricks for things that survive a bed-slinger. Clearance budgets, sacrificial layers, the dance between bridging and overhangs.
  • Defensive disclosure vs. license clauses for patent-troll protection. Public Onshape + dated commits is probably doing most of the work; share-alike does the rest. Or does it?

Built on the shoulders of

This card is glued together from a stack of OSS. In rough order of how much I'd miss them:

build123d Apache 2.0 Geometry kernel on top of OpenCascade. Every cardlab subcommand sits on this.
cascadio MIT STEP → colored GLB in one call. Preserves the per-part colors Onshape assigns; everything else I tried lost them.
trimesh MIT STL slicing, FFT-decoding of tooth counts, mesh roundtrips, the bounding-box math behind every render.
OpenSCAD GPL-2.0 Headless rendering of per-part PNGs and exploded-view frames. Called via subprocess; no library linking, so the GPL stays out of the Python.
Pillow HPND GIF assembly for the exploded animation.
Click BSD-3 CLI plumbing for both cardlab and explainers.
drawsvg MIT SVG diagrams in the explainer pages — the gear chain you saw up above.
uv Apache/MIT Packaging, lockfile, the whole reproducible-environment story. Replaced about three of my old wrappers.
Onshape proprietary · free public docs The parametric design surface. Free public documents made this whole sharing model possible on a hobbyist budget.
Fraunces OFL 1.1 The serif you're reading. Variable axis everything; the headline weight is custom-tuned per heading.
